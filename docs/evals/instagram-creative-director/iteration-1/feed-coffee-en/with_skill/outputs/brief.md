# Creative: "It's Coffee. It Drinks Like a Flower." — Instagram Feed Post (4:5, 1080×1350)

Client: Specialty coffee roastery, Lisbon
Deliverable: Single feed post announcing a new single-origin — washed Ethiopian Yirgacheffe (floral / citrus)
Mode: Executable creative brief (no render) — any designer or image tool can produce this without asking a single follow-up question.
Output language: English (detected from the brief)

---

## 1. Market research → Strategic insight

Grounded in live research (Reddit r/Coffee, r/pourover, roaster blogs, specialty marketing sources), two truths matter:

**Truth A — the product is counter-intuitive.** Washed Yirgacheffe is described by drinkers and roasters in almost identical words: *"floral, tea-like, jasmine, bergamot, shimmery citrus, clean, light body."* The natural process tastes like blueberry jam; the *washed* one is the elegant, tea-like, perfume-y one. Crucially: this is **not what most people think "coffee" tastes like.** The mass mental model of coffee is dark, bitter, roasty, chocolatey. Washed Yirgacheffe breaks that category expectation — it's closer to a fragrance or a fine white wine than to a burnt espresso. That gap *is* the story.

**Truth B — the category's content is a sea of sameness.** Specialty roasteries on Instagram nearly all post the same thing: an overhead flatlay of a cup with latte art, beans scattered on a board, and a caption that reads "New beans in! 🌱 Ethiopia Yirgacheffe now available." Marketing sources confirm "special release" / "limited drop" framing drives retail sales and saves — but almost nobody *art-directs* the announcement. The white space is obvious: **stop photographing the cup. Treat the bean drop like a fragrance launch or a wine vintage release.**

```
DOR (pain):        "Every coffee post looks the same, and 'fruity coffee' sounds
                    like a gimmick — I expect coffee to taste like coffee."
DESEJO (desire):    To feel like an insider in a refined ritual; to be surprised;
                    to drink something that signals taste, not just caffeine.

                    coffee at all — bergamot, jasmine, lemon zest — and frame the
                    release like a perfume's note pyramid, not a café flatlay.
PROMESSA (promise): A cup that drinks like a flower. Proof that 'washed Ethiopian'

POSICIONAMENTO:     In a feed of brown flatlays and latte-art selfies, this is a
                    typographic fragrance launch — editorial, restrained, unmistakably
                    not a template. It belongs in a magazine, not a café story.
```

If this paragraph weren't convincing, I'd research more. It is the foundation — every copy and art decision below derives from it.

## 2. Big idea

**"The Note Pyramid."** Borrow the structure the fragrance world uses to communicate a scent — top / heart / base notes — and apply it to the cup. The hero of the post is not a photo of coffee; it is the *tasting notes themselves*, set as a high-contrast editorial typographic stack, the way a perfume house announces a launch. One line reframes the whole thing: *it's coffee.* The surprise is the payoff.

Conceptual one-liner the whole piece has to earn: **"It's coffee. It drinks like a flower."**

## 3. Copy (English)

Consciousness level: bridging *problem-aware* (the curious drinker who assumes "fruity coffee" is a gimmick) and *product-aware* (the third-wave regular who already knows single origins). The hook confronts the category expectation so both stop scrolling.

**Hook (the hero, the first thing the eye reads):**
> BERGAMOT.
> JASMINE.
> LEMON ZEST.
>
> *(it's coffee.)*

The three notes are set huge; "(it's coffee.)" is small and dry underneath — the punchline that recontextualises everything above it. That tension *is* the scroll-stopper.

**Supporting / origin line (small caps label, secondary tier):**
> ETHIOPIA · YIRGACHEFFE · WASHED PROCESS — single-origin, limited release

## 4. Art direction (premium, anti-template)

**Visual concept (one sentence):** A fragrance-launch poster for a coffee — restrained editorial typography on washed parchment, where the tasting notes *are* the image, deliberately refusing every coffee-shop visual cliché.

**Strategic decision — break the niche's visual code on purpose.** The niche code is brown tones + cup flatlay + latte art. We abandon all three. No cup. No beans scattered on wood. No brown. The piece earns attention precisely *because* it looks like it wandered in from a perfume campaign. (Reference: Aesop / Le Labo product cards, Kinfolk editorial spreads, a fine-wine vintage announcement.)

**Layout (4:5 portrait, asymmetric, one unmistakable focal point):**
- Generous top margin — the piece breathes; ~18% of the canvas is empty at the top. Space is the luxury signal.
- **Tier 1 (hero, top-third dominant):** the three notes — `BERGAMOT.` / `JASMINE.` / `LEMON ZEST.` — left-aligned, stacked, set in a high-contrast display serif at a commanding size. Strong size step-down is *not* used between the three notes; they read as one block (the "accord"). Left-aligned, not centered — centering would kill the editorial tension.
- Directly beneath, small and quiet: `(it's coffee.)` in the grotesque, lowercase, in the citrus accent or muted ink. This is the focal twist — small type after huge type creates the punch.
- **Tier 2 (mid):** a thin hairline rule, then the origin label in tracked-out small caps: `ETHIOPIA · YIRGACHEFFE · WASHED`. Spacing wide, weight light — feels like a luxury product spec line.
- **Tier 3 (lower):** the mechanism line `Not roasted dark to hide it. Roasted light to let it bloom.` in a refined italic of the serif — one elegant line, intentionally the only italic in the piece.
- **Bottom band:** the CTA in the grotesque (small, confident, single line) on the left; the roastery wordmark / small monogram bottom-left or bottom-right. Keep the very bottom calm — it reads cleanly as a grid thumbnail.
- **One — and only one — graphic gesture:** a single fine line-art botanical mark (a jasmine sprig or a bergamot leaf) rendered as a delicate single-weight line drawing (engraving / botanical-plate style), placed off-center in the negative space (e.g. lower-right, partially bleeding off the edge). It is the lone ornament and it reinforces the "drinks like a flower" idea. No glow, no blur, no 3D blob, no gradient.

**Typography:**
- Display serif (the hero + italic line): a high-contrast modern/Didone or refined editorial serif — **Canela, GT Sectra, Reckless, or Ogg** (premium). Accessible substitutes: **Fraunces** (high optical contrast settings) or **Playfair Display**. Tighten tracking slightly on the large notes; optical-align the periods.
- Grotesque (labels, CTA, "(it's coffee.)", spec line): a clean neutral sans — **Söhne, Neue Haas Grotesk, or Suisse Int'l** (premium). Accessible substitutes: **Inter** or **Archivo**. Use wide letter-spacing (tracking ~120–200) only on the small-caps origin label.
- Type scale ratio ~1.5 (major third / perfect fifth feel): notes ≈ 150–185px cap height; origin label ≈ 24px tracked; mechanism italic ≈ 40px; CTA ≈ 28px. Brutal hierarchy: the eye hits the notes, then the twist, then the label, then the CTA.

**Palette (intentional, niche-aware, anti-brown — each color has a reason):**
- `#ECE6D8` — *Washed Ecru.* Background. Unbleached-paper tone; a literal nod to the **washed** process and to editorial print stock. (Replaces the default coffee-brown.)
- `#1C1815` — *Espresso Ink.* Primary type. Near-black with a warm bias for brutal contrast and a premium print feel.
- `#B6C24E` — *Bergamot Zest.* Single accent — a muted citrus-chartreuse for `(it's coffee.)` and the botanical line mark. Unexpected for coffee, which is the point; it codes "citrus/floral," not "roast."
- `#9A8F7C` — *Parchment Grey.* For the hairline rule and the tracked origin label — recessive, structural, never competes.

No more than these. The restraint is the sophistication.

**Texture (optional, subtle):** a barely-there uncoated-paper grain on the background (5–8% opacity) to feel like printed editorial stock, not a flat digital fill. Nothing more.

**If generating the botanical mark via an image tool, prompt direction:**
> "Single-line botanical engraving of a jasmine sprig with two open flowers and one bud, fine uniform line weight, vintage scientific-plate style, no shading, no fill, transparent background, deep espresso-ink color (#1C1815), elegant and minimal." Then place at low-right bleeding off the edge. No photographic coffee imagery anywhere in the piece.

## 6. Rationale (what a premium client is paying for)

- **The pain attacked:** not "we have new beans" (nobody cares), but the *category disbelief* — "fruity coffee is a gimmick / coffee should taste like coffee." We weaponize the surprise instead of apologizing for it.
- **Why this hook:** leading with three concrete sensory nouns (bergamot, jasmine, lemon zest) and only *then* revealing "(it's coffee.)" creates a sub-second tension — the reader's expectation is set, then snapped. Concrete > abstract; specificity is what stops the scroll.
- **Why this art direction:** the fastest way to look premium in a niche is to refuse the niche's clichés with intent. Every other roastery posts a brown flatlay; an ecru fragrance-launch poster is pattern-interrupt by construction. Negative space + brutal type hierarchy + a single botanical gesture = "agency campaign," not "Canva template."
- **Why this CTA:** it's specific (taste it at the bar Saturday / reserve a bag via link) and scarcity-framed ("before the lot sells out"), driving both footfall and retail — plus a save trigger ("for your next slow morning") to extend organic reach. Reward is explicit, not "learn more."
